# Put Pivots on Bones, Then Audit Frames

An AI 2D cutout animator gets most of the way to believable motion with five groups of rules. **Put pivots at real skeletal landmarks** (femoral head at crotch height, not the belt line; skull behind the jaw, not the chin; a clavicle layer that rises once the arm passes 90 degrees). **Cap every rotation at measured clinical ranges**, for example elbow 0 to 140 to 145 degrees, knee 0 to 135 to 150, and never past 0 the wrong way. **Key the hips first and offset each child joint by 1 to 2 frames.** **Time locomotion and falls with real numbers**: a 12 frame step equals 120 steps per minute, and a 1 m drop takes about 11 frames at 24 fps. **Review every rendered key against a small set of geometric flags**: twinning, tangents, parallels, an arm lost inside the torso silhouette, level shoulders and hips, and straight-against-straight contours. The 12 principles become curve checks: area product sx·sy within 0.05 of 1, no linear spacing, opposite-sign anticipation before big moves, and settle frames that spread out along a chain. The evidence has three grades, and this report labels every number with one of them. **Measured** values (joint ROM, scapulohumeral rhythm, center of gravity, gait cadence, gravity) come from clinical or physics sources. **Canonical** values come from named animation texts (Richer and Loomis proportions, Stanchfield, Lasseter, Thomas and Johnston, Williams). **Convention or proposed** values are studio rules of thumb, or thresholds the research team wrote for machine checking, and they need calibrating against approved frames. One caveat matters a lot. **The Lasseter paper and Williams' *Animator's Survival Kit* were not re-read against their primary text in this research.** Every number credited to them below is secondhand and should be spot-checked.

## How to read the evidence labels in this report

Each claim carries one of four tags. **[Measured]** means clinical, anthropometric, biomechanical or physics data, plus calculations derived directly from it. **[Canonical]** means content from a named animation or art text, often reached through a secondary summary. **[Convention]** means a widely taught studio rule with no measured basis. **[Proposed]** means a threshold the researchers invented so a machine can check something; no outside source exists for it. Where a source was found but not fetched, or the notes call a claim unverified, the report says so. The largest provenance gap concerns the 12-principles material. Web fetching was blocked for that part of the research, so **the Lasseter (SIGGRAPH 1987) definitions and the Lasseter head-turn timing table are paraphrased from prior knowledge, not re-read** ([Lasseter PDF](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse557/08wi/projects/animator/linkedItems/lasseter.pdf)). In the same way, **Williams' walk table and spacing charts come through a university page "adapted from" the book, plus an archive copy that was not read** ([Monmouth University](https://animation.monmouth.edu/instruct/?p=351); [Survival Kit text](https://dn721807.ca.archive.org/0/items/taskav-01/The__Animator%27s_Survival_Kit%5B1%5D_text.pdf)).

## Pivots belong at bones, not at belt lines or chins

Proportions set the measuring unit for every later check, so write each model sheet in head units. **[Canonical] Richer's average adult is 7.5 heads tall.** Head plus torso equals 4 heads and the leg, measured from the top of the femur, also equals 4, with a 0.5 head overlap. Landmarks fall one head apart below the chin: nipples at 1, navel at 2, crotch at 3. The body's midpoint sits about 3.75 heads down, at the pubic bone ([Proko, Richer](https://www.proko.com/human-figure-proportions-average-richer)). **[Canonical] Loomis's idealized figure is 8 heads**, with shoulders 2⅓ heads wide against Richer's 2 for males, and heroic figures run 8.5 to 9 ([Proko, Idealistic](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/human-proportions-idealistic-figures/notes)). Several Richer ratios translate straight into rig checks. Shoulder to elbow equals elbow to knuckles. The arm from the armpit is 3 heads. The femur is 2 heads, and shin plus foot is another 2. Arm span roughly equals height ([Proko, Richer](https://www.proko.com/human-figure-proportions-average-richer)). Children have fewer heads the younger they are. **[Canonical] A 1 year old is 4.5 heads, an 8 year old about 6.25, and a 12 year old 7, with the body's center at the crotch** ([US Navy drafting manual](https://draftingmanuals.tpub.com/14262/css/Measuring-The-Child-Figure-With-The-Head-149.htm)). **[Convention, no source found] Chibi systems of 2 to 4 heads are studio style choices, not measurements.** As head count drops, elbows and knees matter less and torso squash and stretch carries more of the acting. The "rubber limb" check follows from all this. If an upper arm, forearm, thigh or shin changes length in head units between keys, flag it, unless stretch is a deliberate style choice and area is preserved.

The most important measured fact for rigging is that **[Measured] the shoulder does not pivot from a fixed point.** Raising the arm from 0 to 180 degrees takes about 120 degrees at the glenohumeral joint and about 60 degrees of scapula rotation, a 2:1 scapulohumeral rhythm. The scapula barely moves in the first 30 degrees. Between 90 and 180 degrees the clavicle rotates back 30 to 50 degrees and **rises up to 15 degrees** ([OrthoFixar](https://orthofixar.com/special-test/scapulohumeral-rhythm)). In cutout terms, the upper-arm layer needs a parent clavicle layer pivoting at the pit of the neck. That layer should rise about 10 to 15 degrees whenever the arm goes above 90 degrees, and during shrugs. Knees and hips need the same care. **[Canonical] The knee joint sits at the bottom of the patella** when the leg is straight, not at the center of the kneecap, and Richer measures leg length from the greater trochanter, not the belt ([Proko, Richer](https://www.proko.com/human-figure-proportions-average-richer)).

The research notes give several more pivot rules from general anatomy. They were **not verified against a fetched source**, so treat them as conventions. The thigh pivots at the femoral head, at roughly crotch height. A belt-line thigh pivot is a classic cutout error that makes legs swing from the waist and grow longer during kicks. The skull pivots on the atlas, behind and below the ear at earlobe level. A chin pivot makes nods look like the face swings on a hinge. The neck rises from the ribcage at a forward angle, not straight up. The wrist pivots at the crease, not mid-palm. The ankle pivots at the malleoli. A toe layer should pivot at the ball of the foot, about a third of the foot's length back from the tip, so the toe stays planted while the heel peels. Rig ribcage and pelvis as two rigid layers with the bend between them at waist level.

## Clinical joint ranges become hard rotation limits

Measured range-of-motion tables differ by 10 to 30 degrees between sources for some joints. The practical rule is to use the higher clinical value as the hard limit and the lower one as the comfortable acting zone. The table merges the two fetched sources: a WikEM chart citing Luttgens and Hamilton ([WikEM](https://wikem.org/wiki/Range_of_motion_by_joint)) and Enlyte's "generally accepted" values ([Enlyte](https://www.enlyte.com/insights/article/adjuster/understanding-normal-range-motion-joint-functionality)). Both are secondary. The AAOS primary tables were not fetched.

| Joint (2D angle from rest drawing) | [Measured] range | Rig limit and rest pose |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulder flexion / abduction | 180 / 180; extension 50 to 60 | Above 90, the clavicle layer must rise |
| Elbow | 0 to 140 (WikEM) or 145 (Enlyte); extension 0 | Never negative; [convention] rest at 10 to 20 bent |
| Forearm pronation / supination | 80 / 80 | Use a drawing swap, not a rotation |
| Wrist flexion / extension | 60 to 80 / 60 to 70 | [Convention] relaxed hand within ±15 of forearm line |
| Wrist radial / ulnar | 20 / 30 | Beyond these reads as a broken wrist |
| Hip flexion / extension | 100 to 120 / 30 | Bigger kicks need the pelvis to tilt |
| Hip abduction / adduction | 40 to 45 / 20 to 30 | |
| Knee flexion | 135 to 150; extension 0 | Never backward; [convention] rest at about 5 bent |
| Ankle plantarflexion / dorsiflexion | 40 to 50 / 20 to 30 | Table A appears to swap these two |
| Neck flexion / extension / side tilt | 60 / 75 / 45 | Rotation of 80 each side needs a head-turn drawing swap |
| Torso (chest vs pelvis) flex / extend / side / twist | 45 to 50 / 25 / 25 / 30 | |

The WikEM chart lists ankle plantarflexion as 20 and dorsiflexion as 30. That looks swapped: Enlyte and a second normal-values sheet both give plantarflexion as the larger motion, 40 to 50 ([SNCC sheet](https://www.sncc.ca/resources/Documents/Dragon%20Boat/2024%20CCWCs/range%20of%20motion%20normal%20values.pdf)). **Trust 40 to 50 down and about 20 up.** Two review rules come straight from the table. **Fail any elbow or knee past 0 degrees the wrong way** unless hyperextension is deliberate, since neither table lists normal hyperextension. **[Convention] Flag elbows and knees locked at exactly 0** on anything other than a deliberate push or a fully loaded weight leg, and keep a 5 to 15 degree bend instead. Combined motions can exceed single-joint limits. A toe touch uses hip flexion plus spine flexion, so a reviewer should add up the chain before flagging a whole-body pose.

## Hips lead, gravity sets timing, extremities arrive last

Balance comes first. **[Measured] Standing whole-body center of gravity sits at about 55% of body height, in front of the second sacral vertebra**, roughly just below the navel inside the pelvis. **The whole leg's center of mass sits just above the knee**, so lifting a leg shifts body balance noticeably ([OUHSC biomechanics notes](https://ouhsc.edu/bserdac/dthompso/web/namics/mass.htm)). **[Canonical] In contrapposto the hips and shoulders tilt opposite ways**: the higher hip goes with the lower shoulder, over a loaded leg ([Wikipedia, Contrapposto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrapposto)). Walk teaching agrees on the pelvis half of this: the pelvis "will roll up on that side as the full weight of the body is taken" ([Tom Jones](https://tom-jones-5wlt.squarespace.com/blog/2019/1/30/anatomy-of-a-walk-cycle)). The shoulder counter-tilt during movement is a convention that is unverified in these notes. Turn this into a check. **[Convention] Drop a plumb line from the pit of the neck.** In a one-leg stance it should land on or near the inner ankle of the support foot. In a two-leg stance it should land between the feet, nearer the one carrying more weight. Outside the feet, the pose reads as falling, which is correct only for a deliberate fall or the start of a step. Characters with big heads carry a higher center of gravity, so their head tilts move the balance point more.

Weight shifts and mini-shots follow one order. **[Convention] The hips start, the spine follows, the head settles last, and load-bearing contacts never slide.** The only full breakdown found is for sitting: about **18 to 28 frames to sit** (anticipation 6 to 10, action 8 to 12, settle 4 to 6), with a 1 to 3 frame compression when the seat makes contact ([Cursa, mini-shots](https://cursa.app/en/page/character-animation-starter-kit-body-mechanics-mini-shots-lift-push-sit-and-stop)). No source gave a frame count for a standing weight shift. Borrowing the sit timing (about 18 to 28 frames) is a proposed starting point only. Lifts and pushes share one failure, the arms working while the pelvis stays still. The hips drop and commit before the object moves, and a heavy box lags the body by "a few frames" (same source).

Walk timing has the best support, since convention and measurement line up. The research notes give frames per step at 24 fps as 1440 divided by cadence.

| Frames per step at 24 fps | [Convention, Williams via Monmouth] reads as | [Measured/derived] cadence |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | very fast run | 360 steps/min |
| 6 | run or very fast walk | 240 |
| 8 | slow run or cartoon walk | 180 |
| 12 | brisk "natural" walk, 2 steps/s | 120 |
| about 13 to 14.4 | relaxed average adult | 100 to 109 |
| 16 | stroll | 90 |
| 20 | elderly or tired | 72 |
| 24 | slow step | 60 |

Measured cadence for healthy adults over 70 averages **109 ± 13 steps per minute** ([PMC3104090](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3104090)), and **about 100 steps per minute marks moderate-intensity walking** ([UMass, Tudor-Locke](https://www.umass.edu/news/article/tudor-locke-examines-walking-cadence)). So Williams' "on 12s" is a real but brisk human walk, and **on 14s is closer to an average relaxed adult**. Two warnings apply. First, the Monmouth page labels its rows inconsistently; read cycle length as twice the step length ([Monmouth](https://animation.monmouth.edu/instruct/?p=351)). Second, the principles notes recall "8 frames brisk" from Williams, which clashes with Monmouth's "8 equals slow run or cartoon walk". The Cursa run page's "8 to 12 frames per step" for runs probably means frames per cycle ([Cursa, runs](https://cursa.app/en/page/character-animation-starter-kit-run-cycles-and-faster-locomotion-basics)). None of these were checked against the book itself. For a 24 frame cycle, **[convention] put contacts on frames 1, 13 and 25, downs on 4 and 16, and push-off/up on 10 and 22. Render frames 1 to 24** so the loop does not hitch on a duplicate frame ([Monmouth](https://animation.monmouth.edu/instruct/?p=351)). The body is lowest at the down pose and highest at passing/up, and the arms oppose the legs. **[Convention] A run needs both feet off the ground for at least 1 to 2 frames.** Common run errors are overreaching at strike (reads as braking), toe drag (reads as tired), too much hip drop (reads as stomping), too much torso rotation, and uneven strides ([Cursa, runs](https://cursa.app/en/page/character-animation-starter-kit-run-cycles-and-faster-locomotion-basics)).

Gravity gives the checks that are hardest to argue with. **[Measured/derived] In free fall, the distance per frame at 24 fps grows as 8.5 mm × (2n − 1), so spacing increments run 1:3:5:7.** That is Galileo's odd-number rule, not "doubling". **A 0.5 m drop takes about 8 frames, 1 m about 11, and 2 m about 15.** For jumps, a 0.25 m rise means about 11 frames of total airtime, 0.5 m about 15 and 1 m about 22, with equal time up and down. A 1 m fall stretched to 24 frames will look floaty. These figures come from the body-mechanics notes, calculated with g = 9.81 m/s². **[Canonical] The jump sequence is crouch, push toward the destination, stretch at takeoff, squash on landing, recover** ([Toon Boom Learn](https://learn.toonboom.com/modules/jump-animation1/topic/deconstructing-a-jump)). Bounce decay follows the coefficient of restitution e. Each peak is e² of the last and each airtime e of the last, so a lively ball (e about 0.8) keeps about 64% of its height and 80% of its airtime per bounce. A dead or heavy object (e 0.3 to 0.5) settles in 1 or 2 bounces. These e values are typical physics figures and have no citation in the notes. **[Convention] Heavy objects take more force to start and stop; light ones start fast and stop easily** ([Pluralsight](https://www.pluralsight.com/resources/blog/software-development/character-animation-fundamentals-timing-spacing)).

Overlap is where cutout fails most often, because every layer gets keyed on the same frame. **[Canonical] Successive breaking of joints sends force from the shoulder through the elbow and wrist to the fingers, which arrive last**, "the same way electricity flows through a wire" ([Brian Lemay](https://www.brianlemay.com/Pages/animationschool/animation/1stsemesteranimation/successivebreaking.html)). No source gives frame offsets. **[Convention] Offset each child 1 to 2 frames behind its parent along hips, chest, shoulder, upper arm, forearm, hand and fingers**, and 2 to 4 frames per segment on tails, ears, hair and scarves. On a 4-link chain the tip then lands 4 to 8 frames after the root. **[Proposed] Loose parts should overshoot by 10 to 30% of the main angular change, then settle in 2 to 3 swings, each about 40 to 60% of the one before.** Head turns were the weakest topic in the notes, and the key tutorial fetch was blocked. The eyes leading by 2 to 4 frames, a blink mid-turn that hides the drawing swap, and a downward dip through the middle of the turn are all **unverified conventions**.

## Fourteen geometric flags catch most bad poses and designs

Shape and pose rules divide into perception principles that working pros agree on and style conventions that can be broken on purpose. The strongest sourced rule comes from Stanchfield. **[Canonical] "A straight line is the symbol for a stretch, and a bent or folded line is the symbol for a squash."** In any bend, the stretched side should read straighter and the squashed side curved or folded ([Stanchfield, Issuu p.112](https://issuu.com/yonisalmon/docs/gesture_drawing_for_animation/112)). Hair and clothing should squash and stretch on the same sides as the body, or the ideas "cancel each other out" ([Animation CA, quoting Stanchfield](https://animationca.blogspot.com/2014/08/stretch-on-squash-on-opposite-sides-of.html)). **[Canonical] The line of action, credited to Preston Blair, is a single spine curve (usually a C or S) that carries into the limbs** ([Animation Apprentice, Hartline](https://animationapprentice.blogspot.com/2018/02/aaron-hartline-explains-line-of-action.html)). **[Canonical] Twinning and parallels, a fault named in Thomas and Johnston, make poses stiff**. They are fine on purpose for model sheets, robots and insects ([Oliver, Character Design Notes](https://characterdesignnotes.blogspot.com/2010/11/how-to-kill-great-character-design-part.html)). Twinning also covers timing: limbs that hit extremes on the same frame. **[Convention] Offset those keys by 1 to 2 frames** and let the gesture land on the operative word ([Shawn Kelly, Animation Mentor](https://www.animationmentor.com/?p=4836)). The table pairs each rule with a detector. **Every numeric threshold in the right-hand column is a proposed heuristic that needs calibration.**

| Flag | Bad vs good | Source | [Proposed] detector |
|---|---|---|---|
| No line of action | stacked vertical parts vs one C or S | [Hartline](https://animationapprentice.blogspot.com/2018/02/aaron-hartline-explains-line-of-action.html) | spline through neck, spine, pelvis, support foot; 0 sign changes and near-zero curvature = stiff, more than 2 = wobbly |
| Straight against straight, curve against curve | "tube" or "sausage" limb vs straight on stretch side, curve on squash side | [Stanchfield](https://issuu.com/yonisalmon/docs/gesture_drawing_for_animation/112) | signed curvature of left and right contours; both high and same sign, or both near zero = flag |
| No pinch | equal-length sides in a declared bend | [Animation CA](https://animationca.blogspot.com/2014/08/stretch-on-squash-on-opposite-sides-of.html) | contour arc-length ratio near 1.0 = flag |
| Level tilts | shoulders, hips, head all horizontal vs opposing tilts | [Animator Island](https://www.animatorisland.com/perfect-posing-2-appealing-and-readable/) | all within about 3 degrees of horizontal and of each other |
| Twinning | mirrored limbs vs one acting, one resting | [Oliver](https://characterdesignnotes.blogspot.com/2010/11/how-to-kill-great-character-design-part.html) | mirrored-mask IoU above 0.9, or paired joint angles within 10 degrees; L/R curve correlation above 0.95 with the same key frames |
| Parallels | arm parallel to leg or torso edge vs converging lines | [Oliver](https://characterdesignnotes.blogspot.com/2010/11/how-to-kill-great-character-design-part.html) | non-connected limb segments within 5 to 8 degrees |
| Tangent | outlines that just kiss vs clear overlap or gap | [Animator Island](https://www.animatorisland.com/perfect-posing-2-appealing-and-readable/) | separate contours under about 2 px apart with no occlusion; also check the frame edge |
| Even repeats | equally fanned fingers vs grouped (2+2, 3+1) | [Oliver](https://characterdesignnotes.blogspot.com/2010/11/how-to-kill-great-character-design-part.html) | low coefficient of variation of gaps and angles |
| Stiff verticals | plumb spine and legs vs diagonals | [Oliver](https://characterdesignnotes.blogspot.com/2010/11/how-to-kill-great-character-design-part.html) | most segment length within ±5 degrees of vertical or horizontal |
| Arm lost in torso | acting hand in front of chest vs breaking the outline | [Pratt](https://twinsandtangents.blogspot.com/2012/04/class-1-week-5-silhouettes.html) | acting wrist keypoint inside the shoulder-hip torso polygon |
| Stacked joints, lost limb, twinned holes | elbows overlapping, equal gaps under both arms | [Pratt](https://twinsandtangents.blogspot.com/2012/04/class-1-week-5-silhouettes.html) | left/right hole area ratio near 1 |
| Prop merged | sword flat on body vs held into clear space | [anim.works](https://anim.works/?p=28648) | prop mask overlapping torso mask |
| Flat front, action into lens | square to camera vs 3/4 with head and torso turned differently | [Animator's Checklist](https://animatorschecklist.com/en/pose/) | head yaw near 0 with level shoulders; aim actions 20 to 45 degrees off the lens axis |
| Cropped at a joint | frame cuts at wrist or knee vs mid-limb | [Animator's Checklist](https://animatorschecklist.com/en/pose/) | joint keypoint within a few px of the frame edge |

The silhouette test ties these flags together. **[Canonical/convention] Fill the character black. If the action and attitude still read at a distance, the pose passes.** Test at close, medium and wide shot sizes, against cluttered backgrounds, and in motion, building from the trunk out with details last ([anim.works](https://anim.works/?p=28648)). An automated version would downscale the mask to about 32 to 64 px tall and check whether the action still classifies (proposed). For shape design, the circle-soft, square-stable, triangle-dangerous mapping is a **convention, not a law**. Some articles list Bancroft as a reference, but no claim could be tied to his actual words ([Medium, Shape of Character Design](https://medium.com/media-reflections-past-present-future/the-shape-of-character-design-78c66eb97518)). The checkable part is size contrast, "make big things bigger and small things smaller", plus keeping the pose simple enough to read ([Animator's Checklist](https://animatorschecklist.com/en/pose/)). **[Proposed] Flag a design when its three largest part areas fall within about 15% of each other.**

Faces get their own sourced rules from a senior Walt Disney Animation Studios animator. Angle the head away from camera. Open brows, eyes, nostrils and mouth in a cone toward the look direction instead of as parallel lines. Wrap the lips around the teeth and hide the far mouth corner. Keep the white-to-iris ratio matched between the two eyes. Keep the upper-lid peak tracking the pupil ([Chatrasal Singh, CG Channel](https://www.cgchannel.com/?p=145888)). FACS gives the mouth-and-brow recipes. **[Measured coding system] Happiness is AU6 + AU12, sadness AU1 + AU4 + AU15, surprise AU1 + AU2 + AU5 + AU26, fear adds AU4, AU7 and AU20 (lip stretch), and anger is AU4 + AU5 + AU7 + AU23** ([FACS, doc.surf](https://www.doc.surf/wiki/facial-action-coding-system)). The rig lesson is that **a smile mouth swap alone looks fake**. A genuine Duchenne smile needs AU6, which pushes the cheeks up and narrows the eyes ([iMotions](https://imotions.com/blog/learning/research-fundamentals/duchenne-smile/)). Surprise uses a relaxed dropped jaw. Stretched lips signal fear instead. **[Proposed] On a head turned more than about 30 degrees, flag both mouth corners fully visible at equal length.** Wide-set eyes reading younger is a taste convention with no source.

## The 12 principles reduce to curve statistics and render tests

The 12 principles come from Thomas and Johnston's *The Illusion of Life* (1981). Lasseter's 1987 paper maps them onto keys, breakdowns, interpolation and tangents, which makes it the natural basis for machine checks ([Lasseter PDF](https://www.evl.uic.edu/aej/527/papers/lasseter.pdf); [SIGGRAPH HyperGraph](https://education.siggraph.org/static/HyperGraph/animation/character_animation/principles/prin_trad_anim.htm)). **Reminder: the Lasseter content below was paraphrased from memory, not re-read.** Lasseter's key points as recalled are these. The most important squash-and-stretch rule is constant volume, with stretch running along the path of motion. Distant keys can produce straight in-betweens that break arcs. A "moving hold" keeps a held pose alive. Exaggeration means more convincing, not more distorted. The paper also reproduces a head-turn table adapted from Whitaker and Halas, *Timing for Animation*, in which the same two poses read as "hit by a tremendous force" with no in-betweens and "stretching a sore muscle" with ten. **The exact wording and in-between counts in that table are unverified.** Six principles (squash and stretch, anticipation, follow-through, slow in/out, arcs, timing) are checkable from curves alone. Staging, secondary action, solid drawing and appeal need rendered frames. Pose to pose and exaggeration need a plain or reference version to compare against.

| Principle and mistake | Keying rule | [Proposed] review threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Squash and stretch: area change | 2D: sy = s, sx = 1/s; rotate a parent squash bone to the velocity angle first | abs(sx·sy − 1) > 0.05, or silhouette area change > 5%; stretch axis more than 15 degrees off velocity; stretch on near-zero-speed frames |
| Squash amounts | [Convention/proposed] rubber ball stretch 1.2 to 1.5, squash 0.6 to 0.8 for 1 to 2 contact frames, 1.0 at apex; characters 0.9 to 1.1 subtle, 0.8 to 1.25 cartoony; rigid props 0 to 5% | peak stretch at peak speed, peak squash on the contact frame |
| Anticipation missing | opposite-sign move on the same channel or the hips | move > 20% of height or > 30 degrees with no counter-move of 5 to 15% in the preceding 3 to 8 frames |
| Slow in/out: linear | [Canonical, Williams, unverified] halving chart (gap ratio about 0.5) or thirds (about 0.67) | coefficient of variation of per-frame delta < 0.1 over 4 or more frames |
| Slow in/out: symmetric everywhere | asymmetric eases, e.g. cubic Bezier (0.2,0,0,1) for a quick action and long settle | more than 80% of moves with accel/decel ratio between 0.9 and 1.1; some decels should last 1.5 to 3 times the accel |
| Arcs broken | FK rotation arcs automatically; translated IK targets and root moves go straight | midpoint deviation < 2% of chord on organic moves over 10% of height; fix with a breakdown offset 5 to 20% of the chord; watch Euler 350 to 10 wrap |
| Follow-through: everything stops together | children shifted +1 to 2 frames, overshoot on loose parts | standard deviation of settle frames across a chain < 1 frame; parent-child lag at peak correlation should be 1 frame or more and grow toward the tip |
| Moving hold: dead | 1 to 3% drift | all channels at zero velocity for more than 8 to 12 frames |
| Timing on 2s: strobing | [Canonical, Williams, unverified] 2s for most action, 1s for fast moves | per-exposure displacement over about half the object's width with no stretch or smear |
| Timing contrast | vary key intervals, hold where weight settles | slowest/fastest move duration ratio < 2 |
| Exaggeration | push amplitude against a plain version | 1.2 to 2 times a realistic reference for cartoon styles |
| Staging and appeal | see the pose flag table above | acting hand outside the torso mask on the key frame |

Two research tools help here. **The Cartoon Animation Filter subtracts a smoothed second derivative from any motion signal**, which adds anticipation and overshoot with just an amplitude and a Gaussian width ([Wang et al. 2006](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/wang-2006-tca.pdf)). A keyframer can run it as an automatic pass. A reviewer can run it as a diagnostic: if filtering changes a curve a lot near direction changes, the original lacked anticipation or overshoot. The notes suggest a sigma of 2 to 6 frames, which is proposed. Volume-preserving squash and stretch is standard production rigging, documented in Pixar patents ([US7221379](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7221379)). **No peer-reviewed metric was found that scores hand-keyed animation against all 12 principles.** The 2026 AnimationBench benchmark targets video models, and its criteria were not read ([arXiv 2604.15299](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.15299)).

Cutout adds its own failures on top of the 12, and the notes rank them as important as the principles. Joint seams open when a pivot slides. Z-order flips mid-move. Drawing swaps land on non-held frames and pop. IK elbows and knees snap at full extension. Offset keys make parts intersect. Planted feet slide unless the contact foot is pinned in world space from heel strike to toe-off. All of these are proposed checks with no outside source.

## What the evidence does not yet settle

Several numbers the user might expect are missing or weak. No fetched source gave frame counts for a standing weight shift, a jump anticipation, a throw or a pull. No source gave canonical overlap offsets, squash percentages or arc-deviation thresholds. Segment mass percentages (head about 8%, trunk about 50%) and Drillis-Contini height ratios (shoulder about 0.818 of height, hip about 0.530) are commonly quoted but were not verified. Finger ROM was not found. The Guay, Cani and Ronfard line-of-action paper, the obvious basis for automated line-of-action fitting, could not be fetched ([typeset.io](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-line-of-action-an-intuitive-interface-for-expressive-3t3gamih5r.pdf)). The Williams material is doubly secondhand: the walk table comes through an adapted university page with inconsistent labels, and the halving and thirds spacing ratios were derived by the researchers, not quoted. The Lasseter definitions and head-turn table were paraphrased without the PDF being read. Before any of these numbers go into a production style guide as "Williams says" or "Lasseter says", check them against the books.

## Conclusion

The most useful finding is that the rules split cleanly by how far they can be trusted, and an AI pipeline should enforce them with different strictness. **Measured limits (ROM, the 2:1 shoulder rhythm, free-fall frames, cadence) should be hard constraints in the keyframer**, because breaking them reads as broken anatomy or broken physics in any style. **Canonical rules (straight against curve, line of action, no twinning, contrapposto, volume-preserving squash) should be default-on review flags that a style brief can switch off**, as robots, model sheets and flat graphic styles do. **Proposed thresholds should start as soft warnings**, logged against frames the director approves or rejects, so the 0.05 area tolerance, the 10 degree twinning band and the 1 frame settle spread get calibrated on the studio's own work instead of assumed.

A second point is easy to miss. Several cutout defaults work directly against anatomy: a chin pivot for the head, a belt-line pivot for the thigh, a fixed shoulder, and all layers keyed on one frame. So the cheapest quality gain comes from the rig and the key-offset step, not from a better reviewer. Fix the pivots and the 1 to 2 frame chain offsets first. A large share of the review flags then never fire.
